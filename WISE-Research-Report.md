# Part 1: The EU WISE Landscape

## 1.1 Definition & Concept

### What Is a WISE?

A Work Integration Social Enterprise (WISE) is a social enterprise whose **primary mission is to integrate people who are excluded from -- or at risk of permanent exclusion from -- the labour market** back into work and society through productive economic activity. Unlike traditional employment agencies or passive welfare programs, WISEs combine a genuine market-based economic activity (producing goods or selling services) with a social mission of labour market integration. Workers receive on-the-job training, socio-pedagogical support, and real work experience within a functioning business environment.

WISEs have been the **dominant type of social enterprise across Europe** since the mid-1990s. They emerged as a response to persistent structural unemployment and the limitations of both traditional welfare state interventions and conventional for-profit enterprises in absorbing vulnerable populations into the labour market.

### The EMES Ideal-Type Definition

The EMES International Research Network developed the most widely cited academic framework for defining social enterprises, including WISEs. The EMES ideal-type comprises **nine indicators** organized into **three dimensions**:

**Economic and Entrepreneurial Dimension:**
1. A continuous activity producing goods and/or selling services
2. A significant level of economic risk
3. A minimum amount of paid work

**Social Dimension:**
4. An explicit aim to benefit the community
5. An initiative launched by a group of citizens or civil society organizations
6. A limited profit distribution

**Governance Dimension:**
7. A high degree of autonomy
8. Decision-making power not based on capital ownership
9. A participatory nature

These nine indicators constitute an **ideal-type in the Weberian sense** -- an abstract analytical tool, not a set of prescriptive criteria.

### How WISEs Differ from Sheltered Workshops

- **Sheltered workshops** provide long-term or permanent employment for people (primarily those with disabilities) in a **segregated setting**, typically separate from the open labour market.
- **WISEs** aim to operate on the **open market**, embedding disadvantaged workers alongside non-disadvantaged workers in community-based businesses.

**Three Core WISE Employment Models:**
1. **Transitional/Springboard Model** -- Workers hired for a fixed period (6-24 months), receive training and support, expected to transition to the open labour market. Common in France and Austria.
2. **Permanent/Stable Model** -- Workers hold indefinite contracts supported by ongoing public subsidies. Common in Italy and Germany.
3. **Mixed Model** -- Combines elements of both.

## 1.4 Country Models

### Italy -- Social Cooperatives (Type B)

- **Legal Framework**: Law 381/1991 on Social Cooperatives
- **Key Requirements**: At least **30% disadvantaged workers** among workforce
- **Scale**: Over **3,600 active Type B cooperatives**, producing **EUR 2.4 billion** in output, employing over **78,000 people**
- **Historical Significance**: Originated from **psychiatric reform movements** in the 1970s-80s (Basaglia Law of 1978)

### France -- Structures d'Insertion par l'Activite Economique (SIAE)

- **Scale**: **4,510 active SIAE structures** (2023), **142,300 employees** in insertion
- **Four Types**:
  1. **ACI** (Ateliers et Chantiers d'Insertion): ~64,500 employees
  2. **EI** (Entreprises d'Insertion): Real work experience in competitive markets
  3. **AI** (Associations Intermediaires): Place workers with external employers
  4. **ETTI** (Entreprises de Travail Temporaire d'Insertion): Temp agency model

### Belgium -- Three Regional Ecosystems

- **Brussels**: ILDE + EI
- **Wallonia**: InitiativES federation (EI, IDESS, IES)
- **Flanders**: Invoegbedrijven + Maatwerkbedrijven

### Spain -- Empresas de Insercion

- **Legal Framework**: National Law 44/2007
- **Workforce Requirements**: >30% workers in employment integration (first 3 years), >50% from fourth year
- **Scale (2023)**: **22% increase in employment**, reaching **11,691 contracted people**

### Germany -- Inklusionsbetriebe

- **Legal Framework**: Social Code (SGB IX), sections 215-218
- **Requirements**: Between **30% and 50% severely disabled people**
- **Scale**: **975 Inklusionsbetriebe** employing **13,590 people with recognized severe disabilities**
- **Focus**: Primarily **severe disability inclusion**

### Austria -- SoB and GBP

- **SoB (Sozialokonomische Betriebe)**: Active on the open market; transitional employment
- **GBP (Gemeinnützige Beschaftigungsprojekte)**: Not active in the free market; more sheltered
- **Duration**: 6 months to 1 year, with extensions up to 3 months

### Sweden -- Arbetsintegrerande sociala foretag (ASF)

- ~350+ ASF; ~10,195 people in labour market measures + 3,480 regular employees
- Notable: **Vagen ut!** franchise chain -- 13 WISEs employing 100+ people

### Finland

- Act on Social Enterprise (1351/2003) -- **repealed in 2023**
- ~300 companies hold the **Finnish Social Enterprise Mark**
- Broader Social Enterprise Strategy now in effect

### Netherlands

- **Participatiewet (Participation Act)** -- municipalities responsible
- **PSO performance ladder** for measuring inclusive employment
- Target of 125,000 additional jobs for people with occupational disabilities by 2025-2026

### Portugal

- Insertion enterprises created in late 1990s; specific EI policy **terminated in 2015**
- Work integration now primarily through **IPSS** (Private Institutions of Social Solidarity)
- Illustrates vulnerability of WISE policies to shifting political priorities

### Central & Eastern Europe

| Country | Legal Framework | Key Features |
|---------|----------------|--------------|
| **Poland** | Social Economy Act (2022) | Social cooperatives + non-profits with economic activity |
| **Czech Republic** | Commercial Corporations Act 90/2012 | 66 registered social enterprises, mostly WISEs |
| **Slovakia** | Act 112/2018 on Social Economy | 261 registered enterprises; reduced VAT, investment aid |

## 1.5 Legal Frameworks

### EU-Level Policy Frameworks

**EU Social Economy Action Plan (December 2021):**
- Helps social economy thrive; supports green and digital transitions
- Recommends tailored policy, legal frameworks, taxation, and public procurement

**Council Recommendation on Social Economy (November 2023):**
- First-ever EU-level recommendation specifically on the social economy
- Recommends **reserved public procurement contracts** for WISEs
- Calls on Member States to integrate WISEs into active labour market policies

**EU Public Procurement Directives (Article 20):**
- Allows **reserved contracts** for entities where **at least 30% of employees** are disabled or disadvantaged workers

## 1.8 Key Challenges

1. **Digital skills gap** -- B-WISE project found technology adoption lagging
2. **Financial sustainability** -- Heavy dependence on public subsidies
3. **Transition rates to open employment** -- Often remain modest
4. **Scalability** -- Most WISEs are micro or small enterprises
5. **Measuring social impact** -- Frameworks still developing
6. **Policy discontinuity** -- Portugal's experience illustrates vulnerability
7. **Balancing the dual mission** -- Commercial viability vs. depth of social support
8. **Legal complexity** -- No unified European legal form
9. **Aging founders** -- Only 7% of founders are under 35

# Part 2: Every WISE in Ireland

## 2.1 Irish WISE Landscape Overview

### Scale of the Sector

Ireland has approximately **4,335 social enterprises** employing **84,382 people** (3.7% of the Irish workforce), with **74,824 volunteers**. Total income was **EUR 2.34 billion** in 2021, representing 0.63% of Ireland's GDP. The WISE model **dominates** Irish social enterprise activity.

### Government Policy Context

**National Social Enterprise Policy 2019-2022**: Ireland's first dedicated policy.

**Trading for Impact 2024-2027**: Second-generation policy launched July 2024 with **57 concrete actions** across five objectives:
1. Building awareness of social enterprise
2. Growing and sustaining social enterprise
3. Supporting the green transition
4. National and international engagement
5. Impact measurement

### Legal Structures

| Legal Form | Prevalence |
|---|---|
| **Company Limited by Guarantee (CLG)** | ~75% of social enterprises |
| **Charitable Status** | ~88% of social enterprises |
| **Industrial and Provident Society / Co-operative** | Minority but notable |
| **Designated Activity Company (DAC)** | Small number |

**There is no tailored legal form for social enterprises under Irish law** -- widely identified as a structural weakness.

### Funding Sources

|---|---|

## 2.2 Government Programmes Supporting WISEs

### Community Employment (CE) Scheme
- **19,557 participants** and **1,219 supervisors** across **809 CE schemes**
- Government investment of nearly **EUR 370 million** in 2025
- Minimum weekly payment: **EUR 259.50** for 19.5 hours/week
- Many social enterprises use CE participants as core staffing

### Tus -- Community Work Placement Initiative
- **7,500 placements** nationally
- Maximum 12 months; managed by Local Development Companies

### Community Services Programme (CSP)
- Supports ~**400 community companies/co-operatives**
- Based on a **social enterprise model**: organisations expected to generate traded income
- Funded by DRCD, administered by Pobal

### DAF Growing Social Enterprise Fund
- EUR 4 million total; benefits up to **125 social enterprises** per round
- Two strands: grants up to EUR 15,000 and up to EUR 100,000

### EmployAbility Service
- Employment service for people with disabilities, injuries, or illness
- Provides a **job coach**; available in every county

## 2.6 Key Challenges Facing Irish WISEs

1. **Funding uncertainty and short-termism** -- 1-3 year funding cycles prevent strategic planning
2. **No dedicated legal form** -- Unlike UK's CIC or Italy's social cooperative law
3. **Lack of visibility** -- General public unaware of social enterprises
4. **Over-reliance on CE/Tus/CSP** -- Constant staff turnover; programme dependency
5. **Data gaps** -- National Census only recently underway
6. **Scaling barriers** -- Limited management capacity; thin margins
7. **Access to finance** -- Non-profit structures limit traditional banking
8. **Governance burden** -- Disproportionate compliance costs
9. **Underdeveloped social procurement** -- No equivalent of UK Social Value Act
10. **Mission vs. market tension** -- Workers furthest from labour market = highest support costs

## 4.3 Theoretical Frameworks

### EMES Ideal-Type Approach
Nine indicators as a "compass" for comparative analysis rather than prescriptive criteria. Has been the dominant analytical framework since the late 1990s.

### Institutional Logics & Hybridity
WISEs as hybrid organizations combining market logic and social logic. Key tensions: identity, governance, mission drift, legitimacy. Strategies include selective coupling (Pache & Santos) and integrated approaches.

### Governance Models
- Multi-stakeholder governance (multiple parties in decision-making)
- Participatory governance as a defining characteristic
- Sociocracy and dynamic governance emerging as alternatives

### WISE Typologies (Defourny & Nyssens)
Four models empirically tested across 43 countries:
1. **Entrepreneurial Nonprofit (ENP)**: Nonprofits with earned-income strategies
2. **Social Cooperative (SC)**: Cooperatives with community-oriented social mission
3. **Social Business (SB)**: Market-oriented enterprises with social objectives
4. **Public Sector Model (PSE)**: Spin-offs from public services

### Capability Approach (Bonvin & Laruffa)
Three dimensions for evaluating WISEs:
- **Receivers**: Material/monetary needs (income, housing)
- **Doers**: Meaningful agency (purposeful activity, skill development)
- **Judges**: Voice and aspiration (participation in decisions about one's life)

### Social Innovation Theory (Moulaert)
Four dimensions applied to WISEs:
1. Transformation of social networks
2. Mechanisms of collective decision-making
3. Collective learning
4. Community culture

## 4.4 Irish-Specific Research

### The O'Shaughnessy-O'Hara Thesis
The central finding: **successive Irish governments adopted a labour market integration approach** that produced one dominant SE type -- the WISE. This explains why Ireland's social enterprise sector is overwhelmingly work-integration-oriented, unlike more diversified sectors elsewhere.

### Key Irish Policy Documents
- **Forfas (2013)**: Estimated 25,000-33,000 employed in 1,400+ SEs (EUR 1.4B income)
- **NESC (2023)**: Social Enterprise on the Island of Ireland
- **Social Enterprise Census (2023)**: 4,335 SEs, 84,382 employed, EUR 2.34B income
- **National SE Policy 2019-2022**: Ireland's first dedicated policy
- **Trading for Impact 2024-2027**: 57 concrete actions across five objectives

### Research Networks
- **SERNI** (Social Economy Research Network of Ireland): Voluntary academic network, chaired by O Broin (DCU)

## 4.5 Research Gaps & Future Directions

### Under-Explored Areas
1. **Participants' experiences and voice** -- subjective wellbeing rarely centred

4. **Entrepreneurial ecosystems** -- how local/national ecosystems support WISEs

# Conclusion

Work Integration Social Enterprises represent one of the most significant forms of social enterprise in the European Union, with roots in Italy's psychiatric reform movements and France's insertion economy of the 1980s-90s. The sector spans from highly formalized systems (France's 4,500+ SIAE employing 142,000 people; Italy's Type B cooperatives producing EUR 2.4 billion) to nascent ecosystems in Central and Eastern Europe.

**Ireland's WISE sector** is distinctive: state policy -- primarily through the Community Employment scheme -- has produced a social enterprise landscape that is overwhelmingly work-integration-oriented. With 4,335 social enterprises employing 84,382 people and generating EUR 2.34 billion, the sector is substantial but faces challenges around funding uncertainty, legal recognition, data gaps, and the inherent tension between social mission and commercial viability.

The EU policy environment has never been more supportive: the 2021 Social Economy Action Plan, the 2023 Council Recommendation, and reserved public procurement provisions in Directive 2014/24/EU all provide concrete frameworks. The WISESHIFT Horizon Europe project (2025-2029) -- with UCC's Mary O'Shaughnessy as Irish PI -- represents the next generation of coordinated research, examining WISEs as agents of inclusive and sustainable economic transitions.

This research identified and documented **200+ individual Irish WISEs** across every sector, profiled **20+ EU-funded projects**, mapped the WISE landscape across all EU member states, and surveyed the full academic literature. The WISEShift assessment tool sits at the centre of this ecosystem -- providing the evidence base for these organizations to understand their strengths, identify gaps, and plan their development in the context of European-wide sector improvement.
